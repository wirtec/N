6 One UI 9 features you should immediately try out - Android Police

6 One UI 9 features you should immediately try out - Android Police

Original ↗ Google News ↗

English

After extensive beta testing, Samsung has started rolling out One UI 9 for the Galaxy S26 series worldwide.

One UI 9 debuted with the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold 8 series earlier this summer, but the update has only now started making its way to Samsung's previous-generation Galaxy devices.

As with previous One UI updates, Samsung added a number of new features with One UI 9, and since the update is based on Android 17, it brings several new features from the latest Android base layer.

If you've received One UI 9 on your Samsung Galaxy phone, there are plenty of new things worth checking out. Here are some of the best One UI 9 features to try as soon as you install the update.

Try My FanCam for easier video reframing

Keep your subject in frame after recording

One of the best additions to Galaxy phones with One UI 9 is the new My FanCam mode. I tried this feature on the Galaxy Z Fold 8 earlier this year, and I'm glad it's making its way to the Galaxy S26 series, where it makes even more sense with better cameras.

My FanCam takes away the stress of constantly keeping your subject in the frame while recording. After you finish recording, Galaxy AI can detect a person in the video and keep that subject centered when you crop the footage later.

For example, imagine you're recording a horizontal video of your kid playing a soccer game and later want to turn it into a vertical Reel. With My FanCam, you can select your kid as the subject, and Galaxy AI automatically follows them and crops the horizontal footage into a vertical video.

To use My FanCam, open the video in the Gallery app, tap the Galaxy AI button, and select My FanCam. Let it detect the people in the video, tap the subject you want to track, select your output aspect ratio, and tap Follow. Galaxy AI will then follow that person throughout the footage and create a new cropped video.

Customize the Quick Settings panel to work your way

Keep important toggles exactly where you want

With One UI 8.5, Samsung brought a customizable Quick Settings panel to Galaxy phones, and with One UI 9, the company is making it even more customizable.

You can now resize the volume and brightness sliders to make them smaller or even keep them vertically aligned. You can also move controls like Dark mode and Mute out of their default positions and place them where you want.

To customize the panel, open Quick Settings, tap the pencil-shaped Edit button in the upper-right corner, and move or resize the controls. You can also add new ones by tapping Add a control at the bottom. When you're happy with the layout, tap Done.

See exactly which app checks your location much faster

The privacy indicator now names the app instantly

Android has had privacy indicators for a while now, showing a small colorful indicator when an app accesses your microphone, camera, or location. But One UI 9 brings a useful update to how these indicators work.

On One UI 9, when an app accesses your location, the privacy indicator turns blue. If you're not sure why you're seeing the blue indicator, swipe down to open the Quick Settings panel and tap it to see which app is accessing your location, whether it's running in the background or an app you're actively using.

When you tap the indicator, you'll also see a new See recent access option. Open it to see which apps have accessed your location over the past few days.

Add the new Finder shortcut to your home screen

Search apps and files without extra swipes

For years, Samsung has kept the Google Search bar on the home screen above the app dock. With One UI 9, Samsung lets you add faster access to its own Finder alongside the Google Search bar.

Finder is Samsung's built-in search tool that lets you search for most anything on your phone. You can find settings, contacts, messages, files, photos, apps, and more from one search bar. And now, you can access it from the home screen.

To enable it, pinch in on your home screen and select Settings. Tap Show search on Home screen, then enable the Finder toggle.

When enabled, the search bar on your home screen splits between Google Search and Finder. Tap the Google side to search the web as usual, or tap Finder to search for anything stored on your Galaxy phone.

Motion Assist is worth trying while traveling

It can make using your phone in a car easier

Not necessarily a part of the One UI 9 update, but another feature now available on Samsung Galaxy phones is Motion Assist. If you're like me and find it difficult to use your phone while traveling in a moving vehicle, this is worth enabling.

Motion Assist displays small moving bubbles around the edges of the screen that respond to the vehicle's movement and help reduce motion sickness. Google says these visual cues help your brain better synchronize what your eyes see with what your body feels.

To enable it, open Settings, tap Google services, select All services, and under Personal & device safety, tap Motion Assist and turn it on. The next time you're traveling and your phone detects that you're in a moving vehicle, it shows the visual cues.

My Files app can finally handle PDFs on its own

Open and manage PDF files without another app

Another neat addition in One UI 9 is native PDF support in the My Files app. You no longer need a third-party PDF viewer or the Google Drive app to open PDF files on your Galaxy phone.

Now, when you open My Files and select a PDF, it automatically opens in One UI 9's built-in PDF viewer instead of asking you to choose an app.

You can also annotate PDFs in Samsung Notes, which makes the integration even more useful. If you prefer another PDF viewer, you can use the Open with option to open the file in your app of choice.

Don't overlook the other small One UI 9 upgrades

In addition to these, there are plenty of other changes in One UI 9 worth checking out. Now Brief and Now Nudge have been updated, letting users create custom scheduled dashboard cards based on what they want to see and when.

Security has also been improved with a stricter 13-attempt permanent lockout. If someone repeatedly enters the wrong credentials 13 times, the phone permanently locks and requires a reset before it can be used again.

There are plenty of smaller changes too, including Tap-to-Answer for calls and improvements to Bixby. These are only some of the additions in One UI 9, so it's worth digging through other hidden settings to see more powerful features of your Galaxy smartphone.

6 Old-School Pony Cars That Aren't The Ford Mustang

The Mustang is one of the most recognized nameplates in the automotive industry, and undeniably the face of the pony car breed, despite not being first to market. Launched for the 1964 model year, the first-gen model was designed as a low-cost performance car for younger buyers. It proved incredibly popular, with Ford selling a whopping 400,000 during its first year. Crucially, though, the Mustang provoked an answer from its rivals.

It didn't take long before they responded — in the next few years, every big American car brand had a pony car in its lineup. Like the Mustang, these models were affordable, but they also started an arms race of unhinged big-block displacement capacities. Soon, this high-horsepower contagion encompassed the whole segment and gave us icons like the Plymouth Hemi 'Cuda, Chevrolet Camaro ZL1, and Pontiac Firebird Trans Am.

In this piece, we'll have a closer look at these models, plus other early-era pony cars — not just the high-performance variants, but also the base models and mid-range V8 options. Please note: We'll list gross instead of net horsepower ratings, as they were relevant for the time. In today's horsepower currency, these figures would be much lower. With that sorted out, let's dig into the thrifty sixes, sweet-spot small blocks, and homologated big-block screamers that shaped the pony-car segment.

1967 to 1973 Mercury Cougar

In an alternate reality, Ford Motor Company's first pony car would've been Mercury-badged. Although it's remembered as Mustang's posh sibling, the Cougar was on the drawing board first. In the end, however, Mercury's team continued working on the project only after the Mustang was launched.

Unsurprisingly, Ford's premium arm used Mustang's platform, but added 3 inches to Cougar's wheelbase and equipped it with a softer-riding suspension and more sound deadening. As a result, the Cougar was more comfortable to sit and ride in, with the higher-quality interior creating a more upscale ambiance. The Cougar looked the part, too, with a striking full-width grille, hidden headlights, and wide slotted taillights.

The Cougar might've been comfort-oriented, but it didn't miss on Mustang's high-performance V8 engines. Initially it came with the 289-cubic-inch small block V8 that produced up to 225 horsepower with a four-barrel carburetor, and a 390-cubic-inch big-block V8s with as much as 325 hp. A year later, the Cougar GT-E secured the legendary 427-cubic-inch V8, which wasn't available in the Mustang, making 390 hp. The 428-cubic-inch Cobra Jet V8 engine was also available, though it was rated at lower 340 hp. In 1969, Mercury gave customers another option with the 351-cubic-inch Windsor V8 (290 hp), and later threw the 351-cubic-inch Cleveland, Boss 302, and 429 CJ engines into the fray.

Buyers could choose most of these power trains with the optional XR-7G package, which improved on the Cougar's looks with new wheels, hood scoop/pins, and an upmarket interior. Still, it's the Cougar GT-E that's the most performance-oriented, as it was also equipped with better suspension and brakes. Unfortunately, while manual transmissions were available, most Cougars were sold with automatics.

1964 to 1974 Plymouth Barracuda / 'Cuda

The Mustang is considered the pony car pioneer, but the Plymouth Barracuda beat it to the punch by 16 days. Still, Plymouth's competitor was based on an economy car (the Valiant), so it wasn't nearly as successful as the Mustang. Yes, it had some highlights, like a 273-cubic-inch V8 with 180 horsepower and later a 225-cubic-inch slant-6 with 235 hp, but it wasn't until the second generation arrived that the Barracuda truly took off.

Launched for the 1967 model year, the second-gen Barracuda was again based on the Valiant, but had a brand-new design and more engine options. Plymouth added a 383-cubic-inch V8 with 280 hpupgraded to 300 hp for 1968 in the Super Commando variant. Plymouth also replaced the 273-cubic-inch engine with a larger 318-cubic-inch V8 variant and added a 340-cubic-inch V8 to the mix.

The Barracuda finally parted ways with its compact-car roots for the third generation (1970 to 1974), based on Chrysler's E-body platform rather than the earlier models' A-body. This let Plymouth shoehorn in Chrysler's big-block V8s — the 440-cubic-inch Magnum V8 and 426-cubic-inch Hemi (385 and 425 hp respectively). Plymouth reserved them strictly for the high-performance 'Cuda models, also equipped with an upgraded suspension, wider treads, shaker hood scoop, "Slap-Stick" floor shift manual gearbox, and optional racing mirrors. These were some of the rarest pony cars, particularly the Hemi variants, of which only 652 were sold.

Due to the stricter emission regulations, the legendary big-block V8s were replaced with the 340-cubic-inch V8 (240 hp) in 1972 and then the 360-cubic-inch V8 (245 hp) in 1973. The Plymouth Barracuda was last sold for 1974 and never resurrected again.

1970 to 1974 Dodge Challenger

Dodge wasted no time delivering its version of the Plymouth Barracuda, the Dodge Challenger. It was based on the same E-body platform, but with a 2-inch-longer wheelbase and 1.5-inch-wider body. Penned by designer Carl Cameron, the Challenger looked modern and aggressive, though internally it was largely similar to the third-gen Barracuda.

Consequently, the Challenger shared the base 225-cubic-inch slant-6 with 145 horsepower, alongside the 318-cubic-inch V8 with 230 hp and the 340-cubic-inch V8 with 275 to 290 hp. Dodge also offered the same three-speed/four-speed manual transmissions and a three-speed automatic. Of course, it's the big-block variants that did the Challenger's keen design justice. The 383-cubic-inch variant was the standard engine option in the performance-tuned Challenger R/T model, producing 335 hp and 425 pound-feet of torque. The 440-cubic-inch Six Pack V8 and 426-cubic-inch Hemi engines were available as an upgrade, though only with the R/T trim.

To cope with the added power, the Challenger R/T was also equipped with a performance-tuned "Rallye" suspension with heavy-duty sway bars and brakes, dual exhaust, performance hood, and side stripes with the R/T moniker. These were necessary upgrades, as the first-gen Challenger had a very basic suspension layout and wasn't particularly good in the corners.

Like its competitors, the first-gen Challenger was hampered by the era's stricter emission regulations and higher insurance rates. So Dodge discontinued the 383-, 440-, and 426-cubic-inch Hemi V8s, leaving the 340-cubic-inch V8 with only 240 hp as the top choice. A year later, Dodge also axed the inline-6 option, and for 1974 replaced the 340-cubic-inch engine with the newer 360-cubic-inch V8 with 245 hp.

1967 to 1969 Chevrolet Camaro

A case can be made that Chevrolet, not Ford, pioneered the junior muscle car segment with the turbocharged rear-engine Corvair Monza in 1960. It proved surprisingly popular, so much so that it triggered the original Mustang's launch. Ford's pony car essentially deleted the Corvair Monza out of existence, forcing GM to rethink the formula and launch its own V8-powered pony car — the 1967 Camaro.

Developed on a frantic schedule, the first-gen Camaro wasn't a purpose-built sports car, piggybacking on the Nova's F-body platform. Still, it had the engine range necessary to compete with the Mustang, launching with eight different power trains. With up to 155 horsepower, the entry-level inline-6 variants weren't particularly potent, but the 327-cubic-inch (210 to 275 hp), 350-cubic-inch (295 hp), and 396-cubic-inch (325 to 375 hp) V8s more than made up for that.

The pick of the range, though, was the high-revving 302-cubic-inch V8 inside the rare Camaro Z/28. The small-block V8 was officially rated at only 290 hp, but the real figure was probably closer to 400. Built for Sports Car Club of America (SCCA) Trans-Am homologation purposes, the Camaro Z/28 also featured a performance-tuned suspension, front disc brakes, and four-speed manual transmission.

On the other end of the performance spectrum were the Central Office Production Order (COPO) variants, which unlocked factory installation of the 427-cubic-inch big-block V8 that pumped out 425 hp and 460 lb-ft. Perhaps the wildest COPO Camaro is the ZL1, produced at the request of the Fred Gibb Chevrolet dealership of La Harpe, Illinois. Equipped with a lighter aluminum-block version of the 427-cubic-inch engine borrowed from Chevy's Can-Am race cars, it had a monstrous 500 hp on tap.

1967 to 1969 Pontiac Firebird / Trans Am

While Ford enjoyed success with the Mustang and Chevrolet was busy developing the Camaro, Pontiac, under general manager John Z. DeLorean, was working on the Banshee — a radical two-seater sports car. It never materialized, but as a consolation prize, GM let Pontiac build its own version of Chevy's Camaro based on the same F-body platform: the Firebird.

DeLorean didn't want to be outdone by his in-house rival, so he went in a different direction. The Firebird was a more focused driver's car, equipped with stiffer suspension and more potent overhead-camshaft inline-6 engines. The base 230-cubic-inch unit produced 215 horsepower with the four-barrel carburetor — 75 hp more than the 230-cubic-inch Camaro. In 1968 it was replaced with a larger 250-cubic-inch unit that also made 215 hp. At the opposite end of the spectrum, Pontiac employed the big-block 400-cubic-inch V8. Available in several power outputs, the massive V8 offered 325 to 340 hp in the regular Firebirds and 335 to 345 hp with the Trans Am package.

Although the name suggests that Trans Am-equipped cars were homologation specials, their engines were actually too big to qualify for the championship. Instead, Pontiac bought the naming rights from the SCCA, creating one of the most renowned pony-car nameplates. The Trans Am package also carried cachet, as it brought cosmetic and aerodynamic improvements, like a big rear spoiler and ram air induction ducts. The Ride and Handling package was also included, featuring a heavy-duty suspension with hydraulic dampers, variable-ratio power steering, and front disc brakes.

1968 to 1974 AMC Javelin

The pony-car craze was so infectious during the 1960s that it even spread to American Motors Corporation, perhaps the most conservative automaker of the time. But there was nothing conservative about the Javelin, AMC's first pony car. Yes, it was based on the Rambler American, but unlike its rivals, it was built with lightness and agile handling in mind. Heck, AMC even launched a lighter AMX model with a 12-inch-shorter wheelbase and two seats, aimed at driving enthusiasts.

Initially, the Javelin was offered with 258-cubic-inch (225 hp) and 343-cubic-inch (235 hp) V8s, but AMC later offered a larger 390-cubic-inch (315 hp) V8 with the performance-oriented Go Package. For second-gen model, which arrived for 1971, AMC went a step further and introduced an even larger 401-cubic-inch V8 with 330 hp. This brought the Javelin closer to its rivals' high-performance variants, though the Javelin was also heavier than before. The AMX variant now had four instead of two seats, but it was still a V8-only affair. Buyers who wanted tidier handling could again opt for the Go Package, which included a Twin-Grip limited-slip differential, cowl induction, dual exhaust, front disc brakes, fiberglass-belted wide-tread tires, and mag-style wheels.

The second-gen Javelin AMX also brought AMC success at Trans-Am, capturing seven of the 10 races during the 1971 season to capture the manufacturer's championship, and then repeating as champ in 1972. Still, from 1972, like each of its rivals, AMC detuned Javelin's engines to comply with the stricter emission regulations, with the Javelin discontinued after the 1974 model year.

English

Apple says it is changing its macOS privacy settings to stop third-party app developers from misusing them to access message histories.

Friday’s announcement comes two weeks after tech columnist Jason Aten said that Meta’s new general-purpose AI agent Muse sent him an unsolicited notification referencing a thread between him and a co-worker over Apple Messages. Aten said he never granted Muse permissions to read his messages and had assumed they were off-limits. Social media last week blew up with masses of people who agreed and said the incident showed that AI assistants given access to calendars, emails, messages, shopping accounts, and other resources are akin to a skill saw or other power tool. While potentially useful, they can do real damage if not used carefully.

He said/she said

Meta CTO David Singleton joined the fray with a rebuttal that appeared solid. For Muse to access Apple Messages, a user must manually give it two privileges. One is full-disk access, a macOS system-level permission. The other is to enable a Messages connector setting in Muse.

“The Messages integration in the Muse Mac app is opt in,” Singleton said. “Your Muse can only read Messages content if macOS system-level Full Disk Access is granted and the Messages connector is enabled.”

Singleton’s implication was clear. Muse could have read Aten’s Messages communications only if he had enabled both settings, and if so, the columnist had only himself—and certainly not Meta—to blame.

Earlier this week, I spoke to macOS security expert Patrick Wardle, who questioned Singleton’s denial. His reasoning: “From a technical point of view, with FDA (full-disk access), any (non-root file), is readable, browsing history, browser cookies, chats, etc etc etc.” I asked Meta how Muse couldn’t read messages when the app had full disk access, while every other app with that privilege could. Meta PR’s only response was to requote Singleton saying: “The Messages integration in the Muse Mac App is opt-in. Your Muse can only read Messages content if macOS system-level Full Disk Access is granted and the Messages connector is enabled.”
